(002) The Architecture of a Cable Robot

In my previous writeup, I talked about some of the earliest thoughts I had
towards making a parallel cable robot. Long story short, I wanted to push the
limits of what's possible with such a system, thought a lot about the cable
dynamics, and then got my hands on a massive capacitor bank before graduating
that fixes a lot of my power availability issues.

After graduating and finding myself with more time, I first went through my old
notes and compiled where my design was at, even if patchwork, and then reasses
how realisitic it was or how I could do better. Roughly speaking, I had been my
system would have a motion range of 1m, have a total inertial mass of 5kg, and
would be capable of a max speed of 100m/s. I opted to increase the size of the
gantry from a 1m cube to a 2m cube, yielding a significantly improved zone of
workability. After all, the angles involved as the payload approaches the walls
inherently means less accelerating force can be applied. It also means that the
mass has a longer distance to accelerate and decelerate, allowing for more
impressive velocity numbers. That being said, while I do want to play a bit of
specifications game, getting to that velocity number really means having an
impressive acceleration number, which is where the actual engineering challenge
applies.

After my gantry size was determined, I wanted to start to bound the problem
a bit more. To begin, I assumed that the power requirements of such a system
would be the major limitation. I also considered that such a gantry system is
inherently largely conservative--certainly it takes energy to accelerate a mass
to a considerable velocity, but that energy is returned to storage when the
motors regenerate the kinetic energy. Obviously, there will be losses--aero,
magnetic, conduction, switching, leakage to name a few--and these will have to
compensated for by pulling power from the grid. Thus, the useful movements of a
high-speed cable robot are limited by the losses overly discharging the
ultracapacitor bank faster than the grid converter can keep it topped up.

Next up is the question of moving power around the system. Fundamentally to
achieve the specs I want I need to make sure that I have the energy and power
requirements satisfied. Gravity is much less aggressive than the numbers I wish
to achieve, so I'll neglect it for now. The longest path I have available in a
2m cube is travelling from one corner to the opposite corner, 2sqrt(3)m away.
If I want to hit 100m/s just for a moment with a linear acceleration profile,
the payload needs to go from 0m/s all the way to 100m/s over sqrt(3)m, half of
the diagonal distance. Referring to high-school physics,
v_f^2 = v_0^2 + 2a[Delta]x; v_0 = 0
a = v_f^2/2/([Delta]x) = 2887m/s/s = 295x Earth's Gravity

I can't say I have any human experience on what 295x Earth's gravity would feel
like, but my engineer brain can find that it's about what I would experience if
the London Eye spun once a second and I were a poor fella on that ride. Ow.
Another metric that might be relevant is the record for "highest g-force
endured - non-voluntary" at 214g. To be fair to the cable robot though, I would
be a bit heavy for the 5kg payload limit I originally envisioned. Speaking of
the payload limit, how much power does it even take to sling around that mass?
F = ma, W = F[Delta]x, dW/dt = P = Fv, P = mav P = 1.44MW

That's a big number, although to be fair it's only going to be for a brief
moment. The problem with this though is (neglecting wall power) the ultracaps
need to provide all that energy, and then immediately suck it up during regen.
I have 44 of these capacitors and in series the 2.7V rating means that the bank
can only be charged up to 118.8V, and in practice I'd run it with some overhead
at 100V. At perfect efficiency and a 100V bus, I'm effectively asking for a
14.4kA pulse out of my capacitor bank into my motors. I'm sorry but even if the
ultracaps were capable of that (hint: they're not), that is a genuinely
terrifying amount of current to design for. Thus, ultracapacitor current limits
the ability for me to get to the target 100m/s. Thus, this suggests that if I
really want to hit my arbitrary 100m/s goal, I could just unload the gantry to
perhaps 1kg or 500g, leading to a more possible 2880A or 1440A peak current.
Still large numbers, but now actually possible with my ultracapacitor bank.

So power is annoying, but with some optimizations I can squeeze by during a
speed run for the fun of it. What's still problematic are those currents,
especially that during the worst-case scenario one motor sees the brunt of it.
Say a 100ms movement occurs that ends up being 1kArms during the pulse, and a
theoretical motor were wound with a meter of 14AWG with a total resistance of
8.3mOhms, and the copper losses were perfectly evenly distributed within the
coil. During the pulse, the winding sees 8300W of heating power, resulting in
a total energy transfer of 830J. With a total mass of 18.6g and a specific heat
capactity of 0.385J/g/C, the copper winding sees a 113C rise. In theory this
could work with enough cooling time between pulses, but what this napkin math
tells me is that finding/designing a motor for such high currents is going to
be tough. While I haven't designed a motor controller before, I have absolutely
designed some rather high current solid-state-transformer components and I know
getting switches able to pass that amount of current is also problematic.
As a final point, despite the 68F capacitance at 100V, the ultracapacitor
bus will sag quite a bit and will make mess of the motor dynamics.

Because of the difficulty of handling such currents without ridiculous losses
and component choice, it makes architectural sense to boost the ultracapacitor
bus up to a higher bus voltage, and utilize the lovely wide-bandgap parts that
are both reasonably accessible and something I routinely design with already.
Because the motor controllers will regenerate energy back to the ultracapacitor
bus, a converter that connects the two busses needs to be bidirectional.
The catch with this is that the capacitors on the higher voltage bus can't be
too large, because that means cost; I can minimize the capacitance needed the
the faster the bidirectional converter is, and given that I'm the one planning
the motion of the gantry the anticipated energy requirements can be fed into
the commanded power of that DC/DC converter. This, however, will probably
require a training process because I can not anticipate many of the losses this
cable robot will have. I've found 1200V SiCFET parts are just about best in
terms of price to performance, and given that this bidirectional converter sees
brutal low-side currents, I think the best way to manage such a system is a
two-level multiphase synchronous buck/boost converter. Both the low and high
side switches need to see the higher voltage plus any ringing that may occur,
so an 800Vdc bus seems to be a reasonable first ballpark, especially with my
own experience in designing +/-750V systems and comfort at up to 1500V.

The final power converter I care about at an architectural level is the input
stage from the the wall. While I would absolutely love to design some crazy
converter for this task, I also have to recognize that until I'm doing
sustained movements that wear down the ultracapacitor bus faster than I can
b=pull power from the wall, there's not much need for it. Short to medium term
plan is rather than going all gung-ho about a matrix converter or DAB topology,
possibly even with some multilevel or soft-switched shenanigans, it's better to
just use a simple bench supply or ATX PSU with a boost converter to the
ultracapacitor bus.

The final thing about this whole thing is that I've been throwing around some
rather large numbers, especially in volts. Even though I know how to design for
these types of power numbers and especially how to be safe around them, that
doesn't stop me from being terrified of shocking myself on a high ampacity bus.
Thus, if nothing else I want the electronics in this converter to be as close
to instrinsically safe as possible. Thus, I want to make it a point of having
active discharge hardware at every node that could harm a person, for purposes
of this page anything higher than 36V and/or rails that can source more than 5A
of current. While conservative, these rules are permissive enough for very-low
voltage systems I anticipate using in my design. Furthermore, I intend to have
all rails capable of holding considerable energy to have clearly defined and
obvious methods of assessing the danger of the system. I don't know what this
looks like just yet, but I wanted to make sure that protection is always part
of what I work on.
