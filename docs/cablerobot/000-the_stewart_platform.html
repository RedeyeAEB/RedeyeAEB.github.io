(000) The before times: The Stewart Platform

This project starts in my senior year of high school, where I had a robotics
class that was not particular engaging but had a free-for-all final group
project where we collectively decided as a group to attempt to make a Stewart
platform. Our result wasn't great and I recall that we really only had it go up
and down for our final hardware demo as a trivial motion control case. What
really matters though is that we all learned a lot, it was my first real foray
into having to deal with real-world hardware that wasn't just a cookbook
problem in class, and most importantly got me hooked on the idea of this style
of parallel motion systems.

Below is what the hardware actually looks like. Regretfully, I was never able
to get my own photo but my sibling by chance had taken a picture of it before
graduating. Big note is that most of this mechanical design wasn't mine, even
though I certainly contributed some ideas. In this case, I recall wanting to
explore parts of the project I knew I didn't fully understand--I could have
designed the mechanical portions, but controlling the thing was more enticing.
I believe the rack-and-pinion for measuring position was my idea, after we
struggled to obtain cheap enough hardware on a short enough notice. I think he
had a neat idea to DIY a linear potentiometer, but that solution saw severe
hyterisis issues and drifting. The rack-and-pinion drove a rotary encoder that
the Arduino Mega would then integrate.

The rack-and-pinion, while it did actually work with remarkable precision, also
had severe issues when being run on a controller as primitive as an Atmel AVR.
Even though the linear stages we were using were blisteringly slow, the
combined pulse-rate from all six rotary encoders would bog down the MCU with
interrupts faster than the poor AVR core could interrupt, even though the
total interrupt routine was at best three lines.

To further complicate the interrupt issue was my portion of the project, math.
At the time I had already finished Calc 3 and was in differential equations,
although in honesty I wasn't doing too well with hybrid pandemic instruction.
While I was able to design a primitive control system that would calculate how
fast to run each motor and how to correct for deviations using a proportional-
controller, it turned out that the formulation I came up was horribly limited
by my available computational power. The math I needed to run on the Arduino
was 32-bit floating point, and it turns out that AVRs don't have a floating
point core. Every square-root I needed incurred a ~500 clock penalty, and every
new calculation step would require dozens of square roots. Once the arithmatic
steps were included, the total time for a single step of the control system to
solve would be much of a second.

At this point though, I graduated high school and my thoughts were no longer of
the Stewart Platform.
